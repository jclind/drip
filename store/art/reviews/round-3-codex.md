AI-look scores describe appearance, not how the images were made.

1. Home page

Design: The headline states the benefit immediately. The single video makes the claim concrete. The bottom controls sit close to the image edge, and the illustration occupies much more space than the action that distinguishes drip.

AI look: The layered mountains and overlapping cloud shapes resemble a generic vector illustration. The specific controls, restrained palette, and consistent branding make the overall image feel deliberately designed.

Works: The blue and gold tokens draw attention without overwhelming the video. “One video at a time.” is the strongest headline in the set.

Fixes, highest impact first:
- Captured page: reduce its scale slightly or move it upward to give the bottom controls more breathing room.
- Frame subline: “swaps YouTube’s home feed for one video” sounds slightly awkward. Use “Replace YouTube’s home feed with one video. Use a card to reveal the next.”
- Illustration: keep the drawing, but simplify the overlapping cloud shapes and strengthen the cyclist’s silhouette. The small rider currently disappears into the dark landscape.

2. Lock screen

Design: The two choices are immediately visible. The large dialog provides a clear focal point. The water is so dark that it reads more as atmospheric background than as rising water.

AI look: This has little synthetic polish or decorative excess. The bubbles and subdued background feel connected to the product. The left-copy/right-dialog composition is conventional, but purposeful.

Works: “Done for now” receives the strongest emphasis. The image makes stopping feel like a normal choice. The one-hour sample is easy to understand.

Fixes:
- Headline: “The lock comes up” describes a mechanism without adding much meaning. Use “Out of minutes? Take a break.”
- Frame subline: “A drop adds 15 more minutes” can sound like an unlimited supply. Use “Limits are optional. Use a spare drop for 15 more minutes, or stop for today.”
- Captured page: consider a modestly tighter crop around the dialog and surrounding water. The broad dark areas weaken the visual impact. Preserve enough water to establish the setting.

The frozen bubbles and absent moving light do not make this look unfinished.

3. Mode choice

Design: The three stacked choices communicate flexibility well. The selected blue outline establishes hierarchy. This is text-heavy, and the supporting illustrations are too small to explain much in a thumbnail.

AI look: The repeated rounded cards and miniature interface diagrams resemble familiar onboarding templates. The consistent token system and specific product choices keep that impression moderate.

Works: Showing all three modes supports the claim that limits are optional. The selected option is unmistakable.

Fixes:
- Frame subline: “Hide the feeds, get one video at a time, or add a daily limit” sounds like a list of features rather than three modes. Use “Choose search only, one video at a time, or daily limits.”
- Captured page: tighten the crop slightly to reduce the empty black space below the Next button. Keep all three options visible.
- Headline: “How strict should it be?” introduces a punitive tone. “Choose how much drip does.” better matches the calm presentation.

4. Settings

Design: This demonstrates substantial control, but it is the weakest sales image. Many similarly sized labels compete for attention. At small sizes, the right panel becomes a collection of switches and tiny text.

AI look: The split frame and settings form feel somewhat templated. The concrete controls and consistent spacing prevent it from looking like a fabricated dashboard.

Works: The blue switches and numeric controls make customization visible. The capture substantiates the optional-limit story.

Fixes:
- Frame subline: remove “paid watching.” It suggests money, subscriptions, or purchased tokens. Use “Each drop gives 15 minutes of watching. Choose when your drops can be used.”
- Captured page: enlarge and crop around Watch time, drops, minutes, and dry hours. Let the lower override settings fall outside the frame. Those details dilute the main message.
- Headline: replace the unexplained vocabulary with “Set your limit. Pick your hours.” Viewers should understand the benefit before learning the terminology.

The interface’s own “Paid watching stops” line remains a possible source of confusion. A tighter capture can emphasize the useful controls without making that line the caption’s main message.

5. History

Design: The chart supplies a clear visual anchor. The large daily average and selected-day total create useful hierarchy. The lower statistics add density, but the composition remains readable at full size.

AI look: The bars vary plausibly, the arithmetic is coherent, and the sample details feel considered. This avoids the suspiciously perfect curves and oversized success claims common in generated dashboard mockups.

Works: The blue bars carry the brand into a different type of screen. The previous-week reference gives the chart a reason to exist.

Fixes:
- Frame subline: it says “Compare each day’s watch time with the week before,” but the visible comparison is against the previous week’s average, not corresponding days. Use “Track daily watch time against the previous week’s average.”
- Captured page: add a little vertical breathing room. The history card sits much closer to the top and bottom than the content in several other frames.
- Headline: “See your week.” is clear but generic. “See where your time went.” gives the history screen a more concrete purpose.

6. Tile c

Design: The strongest small-format composition. The large wordmark and blue drop remain recognizable with little reading effort. Its weakness is that the artwork alone does not identify the product category.

AI look: Very low. The asymmetrical curved detail inside the drop adds character without unnecessary decoration.

Works: Strong contrast, generous margins, and a memorable silhouette. It matches the screenshots.

Fix:
- No necessary visual change. Keep this tile. Rely on the adjacent listing title and summary to explain YouTube rather than shrinking the brand to fit more copy.

7. Tile d2

Design: The line adds a useful promise, but the wordmark is noticeably smaller than in c. The tagline also stops short of identifying YouTube, so the extra text only partly resolves c’s lack of category information.

AI look: Slightly higher because a logo with a short slogan underneath is a familiar promotional template. Nothing looks visibly generated.

Works: The tagline connects directly to screenshot 1. The overall lockup remains balanced.

Fixes:
- Tile choice: prefer c for small search and category placements.
- If retaining d2: enlarge the wordmark toward c’s size and reduce the gap before the tagline. Keep the tagline subordinate. At half size it should remain readable, but at smaller placements it will become the first element to disappear.

The set

Order:
Yes. Image 1 explains the replacement feed; image 2 explains what happens when time runs out. That is the right opening. Image 3 then resolves the concern that everyone must use a strict limit. Settings and History belong later.

The search-only experience is described but never shown. That is a coverage gap, though not a reason to sacrifice the stronger opening pair.

Consistency:
The navy background, left alignment, panel origin, typography, and lower-left mark form a coherent system. Caption lengths stay controlled.

The weaker consistency is inside the panels. Image 1 nearly touches the bottom edge, image 2 floats in broad empty space, image 3 leaves substantial space below its controls, and image 5 almost fills the height. Different screens need different scales, but the focal content could use more consistent breathing room. Image 4 feels especially small and dense beside image 2.

Thumbnail legibility:
The headlines, blue drop, selected mode outline, lock buttons, and history bars survive reduction. Most interface descriptions and settings labels do not. Image 4 loses the most. Image 1 still reads as a single-video experience even when its controls become unreadable.

Tile c has the best small-size recognition. Tile d2 supplies more explanation at the cost of brand size.

Copy:
Nothing strongly reads as AI-written. The weaker lines are generic or mechanical:
- “The lock comes up.” Mechanical and unnecessary.
- “See your week.” Generic.
- “Choose your drops and dry hours.” Too much unfamiliar vocabulary.
- “Paid watching.” Potentially misleading commercial language.

“One video at a time.” is concise and specific enough to keep.

Rules and misleading impressions:
The stated screenshot and tile dimensions match Google’s guidance. The images have no visible rankings, endorsements, or fabricated store badges. Google also advises clear screenshots, limited text, and promo artwork that works at half size. Image 4 is the weakest against that readability guidance. The rounded internal panels do not make the rectangular image itself non-full-bleed. [Chrome Web Store image guidance](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/best-listing)

I see no obvious rejection trigger. The main accuracy concern is the History caption’s comparison claim. The “paid watching” wording is a perception risk. The illustrated video and staged lock state do not visibly promise unsupported functionality. The 15% history comparison reads as sample activity, not a guaranteed reduction. Google requires listing imagery to represent the product accurately. [Listing requirements](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/program-policies/listing-requirements)

YouTube appears as descriptive text, with no visible official logo or endorsement claim. Nothing shown suggests an official YouTube product.

I assessed the attached images and checked the published image guidance. I did not run the extension or test the live store carousel. Thumbnail comments are visual estimates.
